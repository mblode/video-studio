# Story principles

The craft underneath the pipeline. The JSON and the CLI assemble a film; these rules decide whether it is worth watching. They are provider-agnostic and medium-agnostic: apply them in the treatment and the beat sheet, before any image is generated. Optimised for short (one to three minute) AI films, where structure compresses and every second is paid for.

## Pixar's 22 rules

Emma Coats's distilled storytelling rules. Read them as a checklist against your beat sheet.

1. You admire a character for trying more than for their successes.
2. Keep in mind what is interesting to you as an audience, not what is fun to do as a writer.
3. Trying for theme is important, but you will not see what the story is about until the end. Now rewrite.
4. The Story Spine: Once upon a time there was **_. Every day _**. One day **_. Because of that _**. Because of that **_. Until finally _**.
5. Simplify. Focus. Combine characters. Hop over detours.
6. What is your character good at, comfortable with? Throw the polar opposite at them.
7. Come up with your ending before you figure out your middle.
8. Finish your story, let go even if it is not perfect.
9. When stuck, list what WOULD NOT happen next.
10. Pull apart stories you like; what you like is part of you.
11. Putting it on paper lets you start fixing it.
12. Discount the 1st thing that comes to mind, and the 2nd, 3rd, 4th, 5th.
13. Give your characters opinions; passive and malleable is poison to the audience.
14. Why must you tell THIS story?
15. If you were your character in this situation, how would you feel? Honesty lends credibility.
16. What are the stakes? Stack the odds against.
17. No work is ever wasted.
18. Know the difference between doing your best and fussing. Story is testing, not refining.
19. Coincidences to get characters INTO trouble are great; to get them OUT are cheating.
20. Take a movie you dislike; rearrange its blocks into what you DO like.
21. Identify with your situation and characters; you cannot just write 'cool'.
22. What is the essence of your story? The most economical telling? Build out from there.

Most actionable for a short: #7 (ending first), #4 (the Story Spine), #9 (the unstuck list), #13 (give characters opinions), #16 (stakes), #3 (theme emerges last, so do not over-design it up front).

## Structure frameworks

Pick the smallest framework that holds your film together. Beats are listed in order; one line each.

**Story Spine (Kenn Adams, 8 steps).** The default for shorts. Fill the blanks and you have a complete arc.

1. Once upon a time (the ordinary world).
2. Every day (the stable routine).
3. But one day (the disruption that starts the story).
4. Because of that (first consequence).
5. Because of that (escalating consequence).
6. Because of that (escalating consequence).
7. Until finally (the climax that resolves it).
8. And ever since then (the new normal, the changed world).

**Three-act.** Setup (roughly 25 percent: world, character, want), Confrontation (roughly 50 percent: rising obstacles to a midpoint and a low point), Resolution (roughly 25 percent: climax and aftermath).

**Save the Cat (Blake Snyder, 15 beats).** Opening Image, Theme Stated, Set-Up, Catalyst, Debate, Break into Two, B Story, Fun and Games, Midpoint, Bad Guys Close In, All Is Lost, Dark Night of the Soul, Break into Three, Finale, Final Image. Note the Opening and Final Image bookend the change.

**Dan Harmon Story Circle (8 steps).** You (a character in a comfort zone), Need (they want something), Go (cross into an unfamiliar situation), Search (adapt, struggle), Find (get what they wanted), Take (pay its price), Return (back to the familiar), Change (now transformed).

**Hero's Journey, condensed (12 steps).** Departure: Ordinary World, Call to Adventure, Refusal, Meeting the Mentor, Crossing the Threshold. Initiation: Tests and Allies, Approach, Ordeal, Reward. Return: The Road Back, Resurrection, Return with the Elixir.

## Character and theme

- **Want vs need.** The want is the external, visible goal (win, escape, arrive). The need is the internal lesson the character must learn. The best endings deliver the need, often by denying or recontextualising the want.
- **Flaw and arc.** Give the protagonist a flaw that the want exposes and the climax forces them to confront. The arc is the distance travelled from flaw to change. No flaw, no arc, no story.
- **Active vs passive.** An active protagonist makes choices that drive the plot; a passive one is pushed around by events and is weak on screen, unless the film is specifically about reclaiming agency. Convert "things happen to X" beats into "X decides to" beats.
- **Theme as controlling idea.** State the theme as one sentence the film proves (the demo film: "the light is kept for someone you will never meet"). Then never say it aloud. Let it be felt through what happens, not narrated. Rule #3: you discover the real theme by the end, so revise to sharpen it.
- **Raise stakes every beat.** Each "because of that" should cost more than the last. If the danger is flat across the middle, the audience disengages. Stack the odds against the character (#16).
- **Show, do not tell.** Reveal character through action and choice under pressure, not through description or dialogue stating it. A held detail (the demo film's final image: a boat's wake straightening as it turns toward a distant beam) carries more than a line of voiceover explaining the same idea.

## Studio development practices

How the feature studios de-risk story, scaled down to a short.

- **Story reel first.** Board the whole film and cut it as a reel before committing to expensive production. In this medium that is the animatic from stills: lock pacing and edit rhythm at zero video spend.
- **Story is king.** Spectacle serves story, never the reverse. A gorgeous shot that does not move the beat forward is cut. Decide what each shot is FOR before you decide how it looks.
- **The Braintrust.** Pixar's review model: candid, honest, specific feedback with no obligation to be nice and no authority to dictate the fix. Solicit "what is not working" hard, separate from "here is what I would do".
- **Iterate, do not perfect ("be wrong fast").** Story is testing, not refining (#18). Get a complete, watchable, wrong version quickly, then fix what the whole reveals. Do not polish a beat that may be cut.
- **Find the emotional core.** Before structure, answer "what is this actually about" in one human sentence. If you cannot, the film has no centre and no framework will save it.
- **Lock before the expensive step (DreamWorks).** Storyboard and lock the story before the costly render. This mirrors the repo's draft-to-final ladder exactly: lock the story reel, then the look, then motion, and spend the final-quality render only on approved, locked shots.

## Applying this to shorts

A one-to-three-minute film cannot carry a fifteen-beat structure. Compress hard.

- **Use the Story Spine.** For most shorts the 8-step Spine is enough; the longer frameworks are for features. Map your beats onto it and cut anything that does not fit a blank.
- **Design the final image first (#7).** Decide the last shot before the middle, and make every beat drive at it. The final image is the film's payload; the rest is delivery.
- **One want, one need, one theme.** No subplots, no B story, no second protagonist. Combine characters (#5) until only the essential ones remain.
- **Every second counts.** At this length there is no room for a flat beat or a pretty shot that does nothing. If a beat does not escalate stakes, change the character, or set up the final image, cut it.
- **Open on a non-black, meaningful frame and close on the theme made visible.** The first image orients; the last image lands. Spend your craft on those two more than on the middle.
