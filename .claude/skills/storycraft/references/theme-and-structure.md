# Theme and structure

Why structure happens, rather than where its parts go. `story-principles.md`
gives you the frameworks; this gives you the engine that produces them, so you
build a shape for one specific character instead of fitting a character into a
shape. Distilled from Craig Mazin's structure episodes of Scriptnotes, plus a
closing section on why craft rules exist, after Tom Vaughan.

## Structure is a symptom

Most structure analysis runs backwards: descriptive work (why does a midpoint
happen at all?) sold as prescriptive work (the midpoint goes on page 55). A page
number is an observation about films that worked, not a cause of one.

Structure is what it looks like when a character engages with a central dramatic
argument. The beats other frameworks name are symptoms of that engagement. So
the question is never *when* the beat goes. It is *why* this beat happens to
this person. If you cannot answer why, moving it will not save it.

## Theme is an argument

**A topic is not a theme.** "Brotherhood" is a topic. "Humanity stands stronger
together" is a theme, because it is a declarative statement a reasonable person
could disagree with. If nobody can argue the other side, you have a subject, and
the film will feel empty and flat however well it is shot.

- **Quality of argument does not predict quality of film.** Very simple
  arguments carry great stories, obscure ones tend to alienate, and execution is
  the variable that matters.
- **Do not start from the argument.** Start from the idea, then ask what central
  dramatic argument fits it, and think ironically: the argument that costs this
  character the most is the one worth running.
- **One film, one argument.** State it as a sentence someone could refuse.

## The dialectic

Thesis, antithesis, synthesis. Every scene opens on a truth, something
challenges it, and the truth is modified by the end of the scene. Your job is to
keep firing antithesis at the character's thesis.

A story runs three changes at once: **internal** (erratic, belief lurches and
relapses and holds), **interpersonal** (the conventional structure shape, the one
the beat names describe), and **plot** (a straight line, events proceed). When
people say a second act sags, they usually mean plot is advancing while the
internal argument has stalled.

## The shape that falls out of it

Read these as consequences, not as a checklist with page counts.

- **Stasis.** The protagonist opens in a world they can live with, embodying
  ignorance of the theme. They believe its opposite.
- **Inciting incident.** Its purpose is to make stasis impossible and force a
  choice. Engineer it to break *this* character. You are God here, and you are
  going to destroy them.
- **Refusal.** They try immediately to get back to stasis. On some level every
  protagonist is a coward, and that fear is what makes them relatable.
- **Act two, first move.** Reinforce the anti-theme. Put things in their path
  that make going backwards look correct.
- **The element of doubt.** Introduce a character or object that embodies the
  theme, attractive because the hero is rational enough to see a better way.
  Fear is the only thing separating them from it. Running out of road in act two
  is what happens when you advance plot here instead of this.
- **Midpoint.** The character glimpses that their side of the argument may not
  explain the world. "Have I been living a lie?"
- **Reversal.** The moment they take the bait, punish them for it, so they
  entrench further in the anti-theme. Do not disappoint the character, punish
  them. Do not just stab, twist. Irony throughout.
- **The darkness.** They realise their limit is thematic, not physical. Give
  them a chance to seize the truth and have them fail: they have lost the old
  values without yet accepting the new. This is the feeling audiences came for.
- **Third act.** Design a machine that tests their faith in the theme and can
  only be beaten by embodying it through action. Tempt them once more with the
  old stasis and have them refuse the relapse. Then make them pay for the belief
  and accept the cost: suffering for it is the ultimate acceptance of it.
- **Denouement.** Show the new stasis. The synthesis, lived.

## Acts as arguments

Model envelope changes the unit. A Seedance 2.5 generation runs up to 30
seconds, long enough to hold a whole thesis, antithesis and synthesis turn
inside one continuous piece of footage, so an act becomes the natural unit of a
dramatic argument rather than a fragment of one. When you write the timestamp
plan for a 30s act, put the beat boundaries where the argument moves, not merely
where the camera changes. A cut onto a new angle carrying the same belief is
decoration; a cut onto the moment a truth is challenged is structure. If you
cannot name the act's opening truth and what modified it by the end, the act is
a montage.

## Arguing without dialogue

These films usually have no dialogue, so the argument travels through action,
objects and staging. The most reliable carrier is a single object traced through
states: the same prop meaning one thing at stasis, another at the darkness,
another at synthesis. It gives the audience a measurable reading of a belief
they never hear stated.

`films/lighthouse/` runs the argument that expertise is worth nothing if pride
will not let it be shared. Its hero prop is a dark iron service key, kept on the
keeper's belt and used by him alone at stasis, looked at and not offered when the
drive breaks, removed and handed over at the turn, and set beside the logbook for
the relief at dawn. No narration states the theme. The key's position does.

## Rules encode a reason

Most craft "rules" are not laws. They are compressed advice whose reason got
lost in the compression. Recover the reason, then be intentional. Worth keeping:

- **Prefer a specific verb to a general verb plus an "-ly" adverb.** The reason
  is precision, not adverb hatred.
- **Present participles are low-energy** and drain a long piece. Fine alone,
  costly in aggregate.
- **Passive voice usually costs urgency.** Use it when the actor does not matter.
- **A scene running long is a red flag worth checking, not an error.** Check
  whether the argument moved.
- **Voice-over is a life raft when it substitutes for storytelling**, and a
  virtue when the story genuinely demands it.

The framing line: if two choices deliver the same emotion, take the simpler one.
The layup and the 21-foot jumper are both worth two points.
